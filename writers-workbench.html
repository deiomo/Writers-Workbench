Main characters normally belong in the character card's description field. Always on makes every entry constant.
Identity

The basics. Goal here is the public one — what they're openly working toward. The private version goes in Personal goal further down; when the two differ, that gap is a free source of tension.

Current status

Where they are right now and how they feel about it. This is the part of the card most likely to go stale — update it when the story moves.

Appearance & vibe

Be specific but also feel free to embellish things in natural language. e.g. Dark brown like chocolate, big innocent grey eyes, wears loose fitting shirts etc.

Body & intimate details (NSFW)

Optional. Fill what's relevant and leave the rest empty. Specific and sensory description recommended.

Presence & body language

How they carry themselves — posture, how they move, what their hands do, how they take up space. This is what the model reaches for when it writes an action beat instead of dialogue.

Backstory

Backstory should explain their currrent personality.

Abilities & skills & weaknesses

What they can or can't do, and where it costs them or fails. Having a weakness creates interesting scenes.

Personality tags

Keep them distinct — if two tags could describe the same behaviour, drop one. Tags are the summary; the triggers below are what actually drive behaviour, so don't stack a trait here that isn't backed by a trigger.

Psychology & behavioral triggers

One trigger per row. Written as When X happens, they do Y — a condition and an observable behaviour, not a feeling.

Core contradiction

Two things that are both true and pull against each other. Example: A healer that likes to fight.

Intimacy & vulnerability

Which way they lean, what pulls them or drives them away. e.g. Core Dynamic: A rough dominatrix. Drawn To: Submissive men. Friction Points/hard boundary: She always wants consent, will stop when partner is actually hurt, but she really loves roughplay. Hates when asked to be gentle.

One per row. A kink with a reason attached ("being pinned — the relief of someone else deciding") gives the model far more to work with than a bare label.

What stops the scene. Worth filling even if the list is short. A character with no limits reads as a prop rather than a person.

Hobbies & interests

What they do when no one needs anything from them. It gives them something to talk about.

Likes, dislikes & fears

Specific beats general. "Hates cold tea" does more than "dislikes bad food" — it can actually come up in a scene.

Quirks

The irrational behavior they do consistently and can't justify. e.g. Character flipping a stray coin on tails to heads and leaving it instead of pocketing it.

Personal goal

The private want — the thing they'd only admit to one person, or to no one. If it matches the public Goal at the top exactly, one of them is probably unnecessary.

Relationships

Format: Who they are — what the dynamic actually is.

Voice & example dialogue

Tone in a few words, then three to five lines that show the voice across different moods — flustered, angry, relaxed, lying. Example dialogue enforces voice far better than a paragraph describing it ever will.

Setting

Where your story takes place, at whatever scale you choose. A building, a city, a country, a planet.

Level of technology

A label is fine, but the useful part is the limits: can they contact someone at a distance, how fast can they travel, what can't they find out, what can't be fixed. Constraints generate scenes.

Overall mood

What's normal here

The most important section on this tab. The model needs to know what's unremarkable in this world so it knows what's remarkable. Cover what people assume, what they take for granted, and what would get a stranger a second look.

Guilds, gangs, families, academies, companies, cults — any group that acts as a unit. Write it so the model knows how members behave when one walks into a scene.

Identity

Size and reach calibrate how much weight the model gives them. Without it, every group reads as either a street gang or a world power.

What they want

The group's goal, and whether members actually believe in it. A faction whose members privately doubt the mission behaves very differently from one that doesn't.

How they operate

Methods, not values. What they actually do to get what they want — and what they won't do.

How they treat outsiders

The highest-value field here — it's what the model reaches for the moment a member shares a scene with someone who isn't one. Cover the default reception, and what changes it.

Who's in it

Leadership and notable members — name and one-line role. Full profiles live on the characters tab.

Rules & code

Internal rules, and what happens when one is broken. This is what makes members act like members instead of individuals.

Rivals & allies

Who they're against, who they're with, and how open about it. One line each.

Current state

What they're doing right now, and how they're faring. Update as the story moves.

One event per entry. The last section is the one that matters — an event that doesn't change anything in the present is trivia the model will never use.

Identity

Trigger words are what makes a history entry usable — key it on what people would actually say when they bring it up, not the formal name nobody uses in conversation.

What happened

The event itself, as it actually went. Short — this is the part most likely to bloat, and the part the model uses least.

Who was in it

Name and what they did — one line each. Full profiles live on the character tabs.

How it affects the present

The only section that earns tokens. What is different now because this happened — who still hates whom, what's still broken, what nobody mentions, what a character does differently. If you can't fill this in, cut the event.

Exports as a SillyTavern World Info .json. Each concept becomes one keyed entry — injected only when its trigger words appear in recent chat.

For anything that isn't a person, place, object, group, or event — a magic system, a religion, a law, a custom, a disease, a currency, a rank, a species, a drug, a holiday. The fields are built to make the concept show up in scenes, not just exist in the setting.

Identity

"Who knows about it" decides whether a character can mention it casually, needs to explain it, or shouldn't know it exists. Key on the everyday word people use for it, not the formal term.

What it is

Plain definition, one or two sentences. If it takes a paragraph, the concept is probably three concepts.

How it works

The mechanics — steps, conditions, what has to happen for it to happen. For magic this is the system; for a custom it's the ritual; for a law it's the enforcement.

Rules & limits

What it can't do, what breaks it, what it costs. The constraint field — a concept with no limits will get used to solve everything.

How people treat it

Revered, feared, illegal, mundane, taboo, embarrassing. What saying its name out loud does to a room. This is the field that produces behaviour.

What it looks like in a scene

The concrete, sensory signs of it — what you'd see, hear, or smell that tells you it's present. This is what the model uses to write it instead of explaining it.

What people get wrong about it

The gap between what's commonly believed and what's true. Gives NPCs something to be wrong about, and lets a character who knows better stand out.

About the token counts

Token numbers shown next to each section are estimates, not real tokenizer output. This file runs offline with no tokenizer library, so it approximates from character and word counts. Expect it to land within roughly 10–15% of a real BPE count for ordinary English prose, and to drift further on heavy punctuation, unusual names, or non-English text. Use it to compare sections against each other, not as an exact budget.

Using it

  • Type the name once — it fills every heading and possessive in the output.
  • Every field is optional. Empty sections are dropped from the output unless you turn off Hide empty sections.
  • The Scenario tab holds setting blocks. They export as a lorebook with a single constant entry — always injected, no keyword needed — so keep them tight.
  • The Locations tab exports a lorebook of keyed entries. Each fires when its trigger words appear — set them yourself for generic names like "the kitchen".
  • The Concepts tab is the catch-all — magic systems, religions, laws, customs, species, currencies. Keyed like the others.
  • The History tab holds one event per entry, keyed like the others. The "how it affects the present" section is the reason the entry exists — fill that or cut the event.
  • The Factions tab covers any group that acts as a unit. Keyed like Locations and Items.
  • The Items tab works like Locations — keyed entries with overridable trigger words. Keep it to objects that have limits and that someone wants.
  • Main characters can also be exported as a lorebook, keyed or always-on — useful for group chats or for a lead who gets mentioned while off-screen. For a character the chat is actually with, the .md into the card description is still the right call.
  • Both card tabs hold a roster — switch between characters with the chips at the top. The side tab Add as many characters as a scenario needs, then export the whole roster as a SillyTavern World Info .json — one entry per character, keyed on their name. SillyTavern takes the lorebook name from the filename on import.
  • Save .json stores the whole roster of the tab you're on. Loading a roster file replaces that tab's roster; loading an older single-card file adds it as a new entry. There is no autosave.
  • Ctrl/Cmd + S saves the current tab as .json.
  • Runs offline. The font is the only thing loaded from the internet, and it falls back cleanly without one.
