Writer's Workbench

Field Manual

How the model reads what you write, and why the fields in this tool are shaped the way they are.

These are heuristics. Different models and presets behave differently. Use them to make behaviour more reliable.

Writing for the AI

01

Context is attention

Everything in context competes for the model's attention. More lore does not mean better writing. A 900-token card that is all behaviour will usually outperform a 3,000-token card that is mostly trivia.

Ask: if the model ignored this detail for ten messages, would I care? If not, leave it out.

02

Blank means improvise

An empty field tells the model to decide when it matters. A filled field is a fact it will try to preserve. Eyes: green means green eyes may come up repeatedly. Leaving it blank means you don't care.

Only fill the fields you want held fixed. Don't fill a field because it exists.

03

The model assumes details matter

Information in context is treated as information that exists for a reason. Mention a revolver under a coat and it will eventually come out. Mention a sealed door beneath the house and the story will become interested in the door.

Don't mention an interesting detail unless you're comfortable with the story noticing it.

04

The model can read every secret

Anything in a card or lorebook is visible to the model. Writing a secret down risks the model bringing it up early. State who knows and who doesn't instead.

For a mystery that matters, state the boundary in plain terms:

Truth: Mara stole the relic.
Known by: Mara only.
Ly believes: the relic was destroyed.
Ly must not suspect Mara without finding evidence.

Gaps between characters work. The model can't keep a gap from the player. Don't build a scenario whose payoff depends on the model keeping a secret from you. For a big reveal, keep the fact out of always-on context until it's needed.

05

Don't write the ending

"Eventually Alice discovers Bob's betrayal and kills him" puts the conclusion in context. Even if it doesn't happen immediately, everything now leans toward it.

Better: "Alice trusts Bob completely. Bob caused the accident she has spent years investigating." The situation exists and the scene stays open.

Define pressures, motives, and facts. Leave the outcome open unless you want it forced.

06

Writing it down makes it true

Writing that Kira is half-dragon makes it true. It doesn't ask for scales and dragon blood in every reply. If the model over-focuses on a trait, reduce the emphasis. Another paragraph about it makes the problem worse.

07

Presets and cards

Most presets set a tone, genre, or theme. The same goes for a rule stated in the card and again in an author's note. It can cause conflicts or produce behaviour you don't want.

Keep that in mind when writing characters and lore.

Writing characters

08

Write behaviour

"Shy, awkward, insecure" is a good start, but using triggers can make it more interesting and specific. "When complimented, she assumes the speaker is joking and changes the subject."

"Hotheaded." What makes this character a hothead? "When someone questions his competence in front of others, he immediately tries to prove them wrong" gives a scene.

That's what the Psychology section is built for.

09

Specific sticks, general slides off

"Likes food" never appears on screen. "Steals the crispy skin off everyone else's roast chicken when she thinks they aren't looking" will. "Kind" does nothing. "Remembers how everyone takes their tea and starts making it before they ask" does.

10

Contradictions give the model choices

A healer who enjoys fighting. A cynic who badly wants to believe people are good. Someone fiercely independent who hates being alone. Two real pulls in opposite directions give the model several plausible responses.

If the contradiction never creates a decision the character struggles with, it isn't one yet.

11

Repeat deliberately

"Proud. Hates losing. Competitive. Needs to be the best. Gets angry when outperformed." That's one trait five times. Repetition raises emphasis, but it also spends context and can turn into a tic the model reproduces every turn.

If a trait is central, reinforce it once on purpose with a tag, a trigger, and a line of dialogue.

12

A want and a pressure

A character with something they want and something in the way generates scenes on their own. One without either waits to be asked a question.

Writing worlds

13

Consequences make the world playable

"The Great War lasted from 812 to 826 and involved seven kingdoms" is a fact. "People from Aldor still refuse to marry into Vessian families because of the war" is a scene waiting to happen.

For every piece of lore, answer: what happens now because of this? If nothing, cut it.

14

Only make entries for things you want to come up

Generic items, a town your character visited with barely any detail to it, a place you're not interested in visiting: none of these need an entry. At the very most, keep it concise.

15

Trigger words are what people say

Key an entry on the words that will actually appear in conversation. "The Stair Mutiny" is what a historian calls it; "the mutiny" is what a guard says. A location called "the kitchen" keyed on its name fires on every kitchen in the story.

16

Write a strong first message

Write a high quality first message to set the tone for the chat. Preferably handwritten by yourself, or edited by you if you used AI.

About the token counts

Token numbers shown next to each section are estimates. This file runs offline with no tokenizer library, so it approximates from character and word counts. Expect it to land within roughly 10–15% of a real BPE count for ordinary English prose, and to drift further on heavy punctuation, unusual names, or non-English text. Use it to compare sections against each other.

Using it

  • Type the name once. It fills the wrapper tag and every section tag in the output, so a character named Kou exports as <Kou> with sections like <Kou_backstory>.
  • Every field is optional. Empty sections are dropped from the output unless you turn off Hide empty sections.
  • The Scenario tab holds setting blocks. They export as a lorebook with a single constant entry — always injected, no keyword needed — so keep them tight.
  • The Locations tab exports a lorebook of keyed entries. Each fires when its trigger words appear — set them yourself for generic names like "the kitchen".
  • The Concepts tab is the catch-all — magic systems, religions, laws, customs, species, currencies. Keyed like the others.
  • The History tab holds one event per entry, keyed like the others. The "how it affects the present" section is the reason the entry exists — fill that or cut the event.
  • The Factions tab covers any group that acts as a unit. Keyed like Locations and Items.
  • The Items tab works like Locations — keyed entries with overridable trigger words. Keep it to objects that have limits and that someone wants.
  • Main characters can also be exported as a lorebook, keyed or always-on — useful for group chats or for a lead who gets mentioned while off-screen. For a character the chat is actually with, the .md into the card description is still the right call.
  • Both card tabs hold a roster — switch between characters with the chips at the top. The side tab Add as many characters as a scenario needs, then export the whole roster as a SillyTavern World Info .json — one entry per character, keyed on their name. SillyTavern takes the lorebook name from the filename on import.
  • Save .json stores the whole roster of the tab you're on. Loading a roster file replaces that tab's roster; loading an older single-card file adds it as a new entry. There is no autosave.
  • Ctrl/Cmd + S saves the current tab as .json.
  • Runs offline. The font is the only thing loaded from the internet, and it falls back cleanly without one.
